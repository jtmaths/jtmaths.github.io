1 Binomial or Normal?

By the end of the course you have two distributions to choose from. The DfE content (area N) asks you to select an appropriate probability distribution for a context, with appropriate reasoning — so the choice itself earns marks.

Binomial $\mathrm{B}(n,\,p)$Normal $\mathrm{N}(\mu,\,\sigma^2)$
TypeDiscrete — counts $0, 1, \ldots, n$Continuous — measurements
ConditionsFixed number of trials $n$; each trial success/failure; trials independent; constant $p$Symmetrical, bell-shaped data clustered about the mean
Mean$np$$\mu$
Variance$np(1-p)$$\sigma^2$
Typical contextNumber of defective items in a sample; number of correct guessesHeights, masses, times, measurement errors
Worked Example 1 — Choosing a model

Suggest a suitable distribution for each variable, or explain why neither is suitable.

(a) The number of faulty bulbs in a box of $24$. (b) The volume of drink in a $330$ ml can. (c) The number of rolls of a die until the first six. (d) The time a customer waits in a queue.

①(a) Binomial $\mathrm{B}(24,\,p)$ — fixed $n$, each bulb faulty or not; assume bulbs are independent with the same $p$.
②(b) Normal — a continuous measurement, likely to vary symmetrically about the target.
③(c) Neither: the number of trials is not fixed, so it is not binomial (and it is discrete, so not normal).
④(d) Probably neither: waiting times are continuous but usually strongly positively skewed (many short waits, a few very long ones), and cannot be negative.
2 The Normal Approximation to the Binomial

When $n$ is large, binomial probabilities are tedious to calculate and the bar chart of $\mathrm{B}(n,\,p)$ looks very like a normal curve. We can then approximate it by a normal distribution with the same mean and variance.

Normal approximation
If $X \sim \mathrm{B}(n,\,p)$ with $n$ large and $p$ close to $\tfrac12$, then
$X \approx Y$ where $Y \sim \mathrm{N}\big(np,\ np(1-p)\big)$
Rule of thumb:   $np > 5$  and  $nq > 5$, where $q = 1 - p$
Why $p$ near $\tfrac12$? $\mathrm{B}(n,\,p)$ is symmetrical only when $p = \tfrac12$. If $p$ is near $0$ or $1$ the distribution is skewed and you need a larger $n$ before the bell shape appears. The conditions $np > 5$ and $nq > 5$ make sure the mean is far enough from both ends ($0$ and $n$).
Why bother, with a calculator? Modern calculators give exact binomial probabilities, so the approximation is now mainly examined to show you understand the link between the distributions and can apply a continuity correction. Use the exact binomial whenever a question does not ask for an approximation.
3 The Continuity Correction

A binomial variable takes whole-number values; a normal variable is continuous. Each integer $k$ is represented by the interval from $k - 0.5$ to $k + 0.5$. Think of each binomial bar as having width $1$, centred on $k$.

$\mathrm{P}(X \leqslant 20)$$\ldots, 19, 20$$\mathrm{P}(Y < 20.5)$
$\mathrm{P}(X < 20)$$\ldots, 18, 19$$\mathrm{P}(Y < 19.5)$
$\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 20)$$20, 21, \ldots$$\mathrm{P}(Y > 19.5)$
$\mathrm{P}(X > 20)$$21, 22, \ldots$$\mathrm{P}(Y > 20.5)$
$\mathrm{P}(X = 20)$$20$$\mathrm{P}(19.5 < Y < 20.5)$
The safe method: first list the integers you want, then go half a unit beyond the outermost ones. Do not try to memorise "add" or "subtract" rules.
Worked Example 2 — Approximation compared with the exact value

$X \sim \mathrm{B}(60,\ 0.4)$. Use a normal approximation to find (a) $\mathrm{P}(X \leqslant 20)$, (b) $\mathrm{P}(X = 25)$, (c) $\mathrm{P}(20 < X < 30)$.

①Check: $np = 24 > 5$, $nq = 36 > 5$ ✓
②$\mu = 24$, $\sigma^2 = 60 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 14.4$, so $Y \sim \mathrm{N}(24,\ 14.4)$ and $\sigma = 3.795$.
③(a) $\mathrm{P}(Y < 20.5) = \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{20.5 - 24}{3.795}\right) = \Phi(-0.922) = 0.178$  (exact: $0.179$)
④(b) $\mathrm{P}(24.5 < Y < 25.5) = 0.101$  (exact: $0.100$)
⑤(c) $20 < X < 30$ means $21, \ldots, 29$, so $\mathrm{P}(20.5 < Y < 29.5) = 0.748$  (exact: $0.747$)
Worked Example 3 — A larger sample

$30\%$ of customers at a café order a cake. Use a suitable approximation to find the probability that, of $200$ customers, at least $70$ order a cake.

①$X \sim \mathrm{B}(200,\ 0.3)$: $np = 60$, $nq = 140$, both $> 5$.
②$Y \sim \mathrm{N}(60,\ 42)$, $\sigma = 6.481$.
③$\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 70) \approx \mathrm{P}(Y > 69.5) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{9.5}{6.481}\right) = 1 - \Phi(1.466) = 0.0713$
④(The exact binomial value is $0.0728$.)
Variance, not standard deviation. $\mathrm{N}(24,\ 14.4)$ has $\sigma = \sqrt{14.4}$. Writing $\mathrm{N}(24,\ 3.79)$ is wrong unless you clearly mean $\mathrm{N}(24,\ 3.79^2)$.
4 Checking a Model Against Data

A model is only useful if it describes reality. Ways to judge a model:

  • Compare frequencies: calculate the frequencies the model predicts and set them beside the observed ones.
  • Compare mean and variance: for a binomial model the variance $np(1-p)$ must be less than the mean $np$. If the data's variance is much larger, the binomial is a poor fit.
  • Normal checks: is the data roughly symmetrical? Is about $68\%$ within $1$ s.d. and $95\%$ within $2$ s.d. of the mean? Does the model give a meaningful probability to impossible values (e.g. negative times)?
  • Question the assumptions: are trials really independent? Is $p$ really constant?
Worked Example 4 — Testing a binomial model

A supplier claims $70\%$ of its seeds germinate. A gardener plants $6$ seeds in each of $80$ trays and counts how many germinate.

Germinated0123456
Trays0139222817

Compare the data with $\mathrm{B}(6,\ 0.7)$ and with a binomial model using the observed proportion.

①Expected frequencies under $\mathrm{B}(6,\ 0.7)$ are $80 \times \mathrm{P}(X = k)$:   $0.1,\ 0.8,\ 4.8,\ 14.8,\ 25.9,\ 24.2,\ 9.4$.
②Observed values are lower at $2$–$4$ and higher at $5$–$6$ (notably $17$ against $9.4$): more seeds germinate than the claim suggests.
③Observed proportion: $\dfrac{1(1) + 2(3) + 3(9) + 4(22) + 5(28) + 6(17)}{480} = \dfrac{364}{480} = 0.758$.
④Under $\mathrm{B}(6,\ 0.758)$ the expected frequencies are $0.0,\ 0.3,\ 2.4,\ 9.8,\ 23.2,\ 29.1,\ 15.2$ — very close to the observed values.
⑤A binomial model is a good fit, but with $p \approx 0.76$ rather than the claimed $0.7$.
Worked Example 5 — Is a normal model sensible?

The times customers spend waiting on a helpline have mean $4.2$ minutes and standard deviation $3.8$ minutes. Comment on modelling the waiting time by $\mathrm{N}(4.2,\ 3.8^2)$.

①Under this model, $\mathrm{P}(T < 0) = \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{0 - 4.2}{3.8}\right) = \Phi(-1.105) = 0.135$.
②That is a $13.5\%$ chance of a negative waiting time, which is impossible.
③The mean is only about $1.1$ standard deviations above $0$, which suggests the data are positively skewed. A normal model is not appropriate.
5 Quick Reference

Binomial

Discrete count; fixed $n$, independent trials, constant $p$.

Normal

Continuous, symmetrical, bell-shaped.

Approximation

$\mathrm{B}(n,p) \approx \mathrm{N}\big(np,\ np(1-p)\big)$

Conditions

$n$ large, $p$ near $\tfrac12$; $np > 5$ and $nq > 5$.

Continuity correction

Integer $k$ becomes the interval $k \pm 0.5$.

$\leqslant k$ / $\geqslant k$

$Y < k + 0.5$  /  $Y > k - 0.5$

$< k$ / $> k$

$Y < k - 0.5$  /  $Y > k + 0.5$

Binomial check

Variance should be less than mean; compare expected and observed frequencies.

Normal check

Symmetry; $68\%$ / $95\%$ rule; no impossible values.

6 Practice Questions

Ten questions, from routine to exam-style. Give answers to $3$ significant figures unless told otherwise.

Question 1

State, with a reason, whether a binomial or normal model is more suitable for: (a) the number of left-handed students in a class of $30$; (b) the lengths of leaves from an oak tree.

▶ Show solution

(a) Binomial $\mathrm{B}(30,\,p)$: a count from a fixed number of people, each left-handed or not, assumed independent with constant $p$.

(b) Normal: length is a continuous measurement likely to be symmetrical about a mean.

Question 2

For which of these is a normal approximation suitable? (a) $\mathrm{B}(40,\ 0.1)$   (b) $\mathrm{B}(40,\ 0.45)$   (c) $\mathrm{B}(500,\ 0.02)$

▶ Show solution

(a) $np = 4 < 5$: not suitable — the distribution is too skewed.

(b) $np = 18$, $nq = 22$: suitable.

(c) $np = 10$, $nq = 490$: the rule of thumb is satisfied, so it is acceptable, though $p$ is far from $\tfrac12$ and the approximation will be less accurate in the tails.

Question 3

$X \sim \mathrm{B}(n,\,p)$ is approximated by $Y$. Write the continuity-corrected version of (a) $\mathrm{P}(X < 15)$, (b) $\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 15)$, (c) $\mathrm{P}(X = 15)$, (d) $\mathrm{P}(12 \leqslant X < 18)$.

▶ Show solution

(a) $X \leqslant 14$: $\mathrm{P}(Y < 14.5)$

(b) $X = 15, 16, \ldots$: $\mathrm{P}(Y > 14.5)$

(c) $\mathrm{P}(14.5 < Y < 15.5)$

(d) $X = 12, \ldots, 17$: $\mathrm{P}(11.5 < Y < 17.5)$

Question 4

A fair coin is tossed $50$ times. Use a normal approximation to find the probability of at least $30$ heads.

▶ Show solution

$X \sim \mathrm{B}(50,\ 0.5)$; $\mu = 25$, $\sigma^2 = 12.5$, $\sigma = 3.536$.

$\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 30) \approx \mathrm{P}(Y > 29.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.273) = 0.102$

(Exact: $0.101$.)

Question 5

$X \sim \mathrm{B}(100,\ 0.35)$. Use a normal approximation to find $\mathrm{P}(30 \leqslant X \leqslant 40)$.

▶ Show solution

$\mu = 35$, $\sigma^2 = 22.75$, $\sigma = 4.770$.

$\mathrm{P}(29.5 < Y < 40.5) = \Phi(1.153) - \Phi(-1.153) = 0.751$

(Exact: $0.751$.)

Question 6

$X \sim \mathrm{B}(80,\ 0.6)$. Use a normal approximation to estimate $\mathrm{P}(X = 50)$, and compare with the exact value.

▶ Show solution

$\mu = 48$, $\sigma^2 = 19.2$, $\sigma = 4.382$.

$\mathrm{P}(49.5 < Y < 50.5) = \Phi(0.571) - \Phi(0.342) = 0.0819$

Exact: $\binom{80}{50}(0.6)^{50}(0.4)^{30} = 0.0827$ — the approximation is good.

Question 7

The number of cracked eggs in each of $50$ boxes of $10$ eggs is recorded.

Cracked012345678910
Boxes228543321110

The mean is $1.86$ and the variance is $5.60$. Explain why $\mathrm{B}(10,\,p)$ is not a good model.

▶ Show solution

Estimate $p = 1.86 / 10 = 0.186$. A binomial with this $p$ has variance $10 \times 0.186 \times 0.814 = 1.51$, far less than the observed $5.60$.

Also $\mathrm{B}(10,\ 0.186)$ predicts about $50 \times 0.814^{10} = 6.4$ boxes with no cracks, against $22$ observed.

The eggs in a box are not independent: if a box is dropped, several eggs crack together. So the binomial assumption of independent trials fails.

Question 8

The masses of $200$ apples have mean $152$ g and standard deviation $11$ g. $136$ of the apples are between $141$ g and $163$ g, and $191$ are between $130$ g and $174$ g. Does a normal model seem reasonable?

▶ Show solution

$141$ to $163$ is $\mu \pm \sigma$: $\dfrac{136}{200} = 68\%$, matching the expected $68.3\%$.

$130$ to $174$ is $\mu \pm 2\sigma$: $\dfrac{191}{200} = 95.5\%$, matching the expected $95.4\%$.

A normal model $\mathrm{N}(152,\ 11^2)$ seems reasonable (a histogram would confirm symmetry).

Question 9

An airline sells $200$ tickets for a flight with $190$ seats. Each passenger independently turns up with probability $0.92$. (a) Use a normal approximation to find the probability that more than $190$ passengers turn up. (b) The exact binomial probability is $0.0374$. Explain why the approximation is not very accurate here.

▶ Show solution

(a) $X \sim \mathrm{B}(200,\ 0.92)$: $np = 184$, $nq = 16$, $\sigma^2 = 14.72$, $\sigma = 3.837$.

$\mathrm{P}(X > 190) \approx \mathrm{P}(Y > 190.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.694) = 0.0451$

(b) $p = 0.92$ is far from $\tfrac12$, so the binomial is negatively skewed (it cannot exceed $200$, only $16$ above the mean). The symmetrical normal curve overestimates the upper tail.

Question 10

A multiple-choice test has $40$ questions, each with $4$ options. A student guesses every answer at random. Let $X$ be the number correct.

(a) State the distribution of $X$ and give its mean and variance. (b) Explain why a normal approximation is suitable. (c) Use it to estimate $\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 15)$. (d) The examiner wants the probability of passing by guessing to be less than $5\%$. Using the approximation, find the smallest suitable pass mark, and check it with the exact binomial distribution.

▶ Show solution

(a) $X \sim \mathrm{B}(40,\ 0.25)$; mean $10$, variance $40 \times 0.25 \times 0.75 = 7.5$.

(b) $np = 10 > 5$ and $nq = 30 > 5$.

(c) $Y \sim \mathrm{N}(10,\ 7.5)$, $\sigma = 2.739$. $\mathrm{P}(Y > 14.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.643) = 0.0502$.

(d) Need $\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant k) < 0.05$. From (c), $k = 15$ gives $0.0502$ — just too big.

$k = 16$: $\mathrm{P}(Y > 15.5) = 1 - \Phi(2.008) = 0.0223 < 0.05$ ✓. The pass mark should be $16$.

Exact check: $\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 15) = 0.0544$ and $\mathrm{P}(X \geqslant 16) = 0.0262$, so $16$ is confirmed.
