1 The Two Identities
An identity is true for every value of the variable, which is why it is written with $\equiv$ rather than $=$. The specification requires exactly two, and they are both on the formula sheet in spirit but worth knowing cold.
PT3 — the quotient identity
$\tan\theta \equiv \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$
PT4 — the Pythagorean identity
$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \equiv 1$
Why the second one is true. On the unit circle, a point at angle $\theta$ has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and lies at distance $1$ from the origin. Pythagoras on the right-angled triangle formed with the axes gives $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1^2$ immediately. The identity is Pythagoras' theorem in trigonometric clothing.
The rearrangements you will actually use
$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$  ·  $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$
$\sin\theta = \pm\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta}$  ·  $\cos\theta = \pm\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}$
$\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$, not $\sin(\theta^2)$. And there is no such thing as $\sin^2$ on its own — it always needs an angle.
2 Finding One Ratio From Another
  • Use $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \equiv 1$ to get the squared value you need.
  • Square-root it — remembering that this gives $\pm$.
  • Use the quadrant, or the information given, to choose the correct sign.
  • If a tangent is wanted, divide sine by cosine.
Worked Example 1 — An acute angle

$\theta$ is acute and $\cos\theta = \tfrac{5}{13}$. Find the exact values of $\sin\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.

①$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \left(\tfrac{5}{13}\right)^2 = 1 - \tfrac{25}{169} = \tfrac{144}{169}$
②$\sin\theta = \pm\tfrac{12}{13}$, and since $\theta$ is acute it is positive: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{12}{13}$
③$\tan\theta = \dfrac{12/13}{5/13} = \dfrac{12}{5}$
The triangle shortcut. $\cos\theta = \tfrac{5}{13}$ means adjacent $5$, hypotenuse $13$, so the opposite side is $\sqrt{169-25} = 12$ by Pythagoras. Sketching the $5$–$12$–$13$ triangle gives all three ratios at a glance.
Worked Example 2 — An obtuse angle

$\theta$ is obtuse and $\sin\theta = \tfrac{7}{25}$. Find $\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ exactly.

①$\cos^2\theta = 1 - \tfrac{49}{625} = \tfrac{576}{625}$
②$\cos\theta = \pm\tfrac{24}{25}$
③Obtuse means the second quadrant, where cosine is negative: $\cos\theta = -\dfrac{24}{25}$
④$\tan\theta = \dfrac{7/25}{-24/25} = -\dfrac{7}{24}$
The word "obtuse" is doing real work here. Without it, both signs would be possible and the question would have no unique answer.
Worked Example 3 — Starting from a tangent

$\tan\theta = -\tfrac34$ and $270° < \theta < 360°$. Find $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$.

①The fourth quadrant: cosine positive, sine negative (CAST).
②$\tan\theta = -\tfrac34$ suggests a $3$–$4$–$5$ triangle, so the magnitudes are $\tfrac35$ and $\tfrac45$.
③Opposite $3$, adjacent $4$, hypotenuse $5$.
④$\sin\theta = -\dfrac{3}{5}$  (negative)  and  $\cos\theta = \dfrac{4}{5}$  (positive)
⑤Check: $\dfrac{-3/5}{4/5} = -\dfrac34$ ✓ and $\left(\tfrac35\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac45\right)^2 = 1$ ✓
3 Proving Identities
The golden rule of proving an identity
Work on one side only until it becomes the other side.
Never move terms across the $\equiv$ — that assumes what you are proving.
  • Start with the messier side — there is more to simplify.
  • Write any $\tan$ as $\dfrac{\sin}{\cos}$.
  • Combine fractions over a common denominator.
  • Look for $\sin^2 + \cos^2$ to replace with $1$, or a $1 - \sin^2$ to replace with $\cos^2$.
  • State clearly when you reach the required form.
Worked Example 4 — A first proof

Prove that $\sin\theta\,\tan\theta + \cos\theta \equiv \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}$.

①Start with the left side and replace $\tan\theta$:
②$\text{LHS} = \sin\theta \times \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} + \cos\theta = \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} + \cos\theta$
③Common denominator $\cos\theta$: $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{\cos\theta}$
④The numerator is $1$ by PT4:
⑤$= \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} = \text{RHS}$ ✓
Writing $\cos\theta$ as $\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\cos\theta}$ is the step that makes this work. Turning a whole term into a fraction is often the key move.
Worked Example 5 — Using the difference of two squares

Prove that $\dfrac{1 - \cos^2\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} \equiv 1 - \cos\theta$.

①Numerator: $1 - \cos^2\theta = (1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)$
②$\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)}{1+\cos\theta}$
③Cancel $(1+\cos\theta)$:
④$= 1 - \cos\theta = \text{RHS}$ ✓
Recognising $1 - \cos^2\theta$ as either $\sin^2\theta$ or a difference of two squares gives you two routes. Here the second is far quicker.
Worked Example 6 — A harder proof

Prove that $\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\sin\theta} \equiv \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}$.

①$\text{LHS} = \dfrac{\sin\theta/\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$
②Dividing by $\sin\theta$ is multiplying by $\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}$:
③$= \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \times \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} = \text{RHS}$ ✓
Strictly this holds for all $\theta$ where both sides are defined — that is, $\sin\theta \neq 0$ and $\cos\theta \neq 0$. Identities are always understood with those exclusions.
4 Simplifying Expressions
If you seeReplace with
$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$$1$
$1 - \sin^2\theta$$\cos^2\theta$
$1 - \cos^2\theta$$\sin^2\theta$
$\tan\theta\cos\theta$$\sin\theta$
$\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$$\tan\theta$
Worked Example 7 — Simplifying

Simplify (a) $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{1-\sin^2\theta}$,  (b) $(1+\sin\theta)(1-\sin\theta)$,  (c) $\cos^2\theta\tan^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$.

①(a) The denominator is $\cos^2\theta$, giving $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \tan^2\theta$.
②(b) Difference of two squares: $1 - \sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta$.
③(c) $\cos^2\theta\tan^2\theta = \cos^2\theta \times \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \sin^2\theta$
④So the expression is $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

6 Practice Questions
Question 1

$\theta$ is acute with $\sin\theta = \tfrac{4}{5}$. Find $\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.

▶ Show solution

$\cos^2\theta = 1 - \tfrac{16}{25} = \tfrac{9}{25}$, so $\cos\theta = \tfrac{3}{5}$ (positive as $\theta$ is acute).

$\tan\theta = \dfrac{4/5}{3/5} = \dfrac{4}{3}$

Question 2

Simplify $\dfrac{1-\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta}$.

▶ Show solution

$1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$

$\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin\theta} = \sin\theta$

Question 3

Simplify $\tan\theta \cos\theta$.

▶ Show solution

$\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \times \cos\theta = \sin\theta$

Question 4

$\theta$ is obtuse with $\cos\theta = -\tfrac{3}{5}$. Find $\sin\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.

▶ Show solution

$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \tfrac{9}{25} = \tfrac{16}{25}$, so $\sin\theta = \pm\tfrac45$.

Obtuse means the second quadrant, where sine is positive: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{4}{5}$.

$\tan\theta = \dfrac{4/5}{-3/5} = -\dfrac{4}{3}$

Question 5

Prove that $(\sin\theta + \cos\theta)^2 \equiv 1 + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.

▶ Show solution

$\text{LHS} = \sin^2\theta + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta$

$= \left(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta\right) + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$

$= 1 + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \text{RHS}$ ✓

Question 6

Simplify $\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\tan\theta}$.

▶ Show solution

$\cos\theta \div \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \cos\theta \times \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$

$= \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta}$

Question 7

Show that $\sin^4\theta - \cos^4\theta \equiv \sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta$.

▶ Show solution

$\text{LHS}$ is a difference of two squares:

$= \left(\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta\right)\left(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta\right)$

The second bracket is $1$, so

$= \sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta = \text{RHS}$ ✓

Question 8

Given $\tan\theta = \tfrac{5}{12}$ and $\theta$ is acute, find $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$.

▶ Show solution

Opposite $5$, adjacent $12$, so the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{25+144} = 13$.

$\sin\theta = \dfrac{5}{13}$,  $\cos\theta = \dfrac{12}{13}$

Both positive, since $\theta$ is acute.

Question 9

Prove that $\dfrac{1}{1+\tan^2\theta} \equiv \cos^2\theta$.

▶ Show solution

Work on the left. First, $\tan^2\theta = \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta}$:

$1 + \tan^2\theta = 1 + \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta}$

The numerator is $1$ by PT4, so $1 + \tan^2\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta}$.

Therefore $\dfrac{1}{1+\tan^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{1/\cos^2\theta} = \cos^2\theta = \text{RHS}$ ✓

Question 10

(a) Show that the equation $2\cos^2\theta + 3\sin\theta = 3$ can be written as $2\sin^2\theta - 3\sin\theta + 1 = 0$.   (b) Hence solve it for $0° \leqslant \theta \leqslant 360°$.   (c) Explain why one of the factors gives only one solution in the interval while the other gives two.

▶ Show solution

(a) The equation mixes $\cos^2$ with $\sin$, so convert to a single ratio using PT4:

$\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$

$2\left(1 - \sin^2\theta\right) + 3\sin\theta = 3$

$2 - 2\sin^2\theta + 3\sin\theta = 3$

$0 = 2\sin^2\theta - 3\sin\theta + 1$  as required.

(b) This is a quadratic in $\sin\theta$. Let $s = \sin\theta$:

$2s^2 - 3s + 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (2s-1)(s-1) = 0$

So $\sin\theta = \tfrac12$ or $\sin\theta = 1$.

$\sin\theta = \tfrac12$:  $\theta = 30°$ or $180° - 30° = 150°$

$\sin\theta = 1$:  $\theta = 90°$

$\theta = 30°,\; 90°,\; 150°$

(c) The value $\sin\theta = \tfrac12$ lies strictly between $-1$ and $1$, so the horizontal line $y = \tfrac12$ cuts the sine curve twice in one full period — once going up and once coming down.

But $\sin\theta = 1$ is the maximum value of the sine function. The line $y = 1$ only touches the curve at its peak, which happens once in the interval, at $\theta = 90°$.

Whenever a trigonometric equation produces $\pm 1$ for sine or cosine, expect one fewer solution than usual.
