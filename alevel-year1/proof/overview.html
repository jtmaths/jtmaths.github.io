🔍 Proof Overview

A Level Maths Year 1 · Overview of the whole topic

Level 3 · Ages 16–17
1 The Big Idea

A proof is a chain of logical steps that starts from things you already know to be true and ends at the statement you want to establish. Once a result is proved, it is true for ever and in every case — no amount of checking examples can do that.

At GCSE you were mostly asked to show that a number or an expression came out right. At A Level you are asked to prove that something is always true — for every integer, every real number, every triangle. Checking ten cases, or ten thousand, is not a proof.
The shape of every proof
Assumptions (what you are given, and known facts)
$\Downarrow$   a sequence of valid logical steps   $\Downarrow$
Conclusion (exactly the statement you were asked to prove)

In the first year of A Level you meet three methods (DfE content area A · Proof):

MethodWhat you doTypical use
Proof by deductionStart from general facts and argue step by step to the result, usually with algebra."Prove that the sum of any two odd numbers is even."
Proof by exhaustionSplit the situation into a finite number of cases and check every one."Prove that every square number leaves remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 3."
Disproof by counter-exampleFind one case where a claim fails."Show that '$n^2 + n + 41$ is always prime' is false."
Proof by contradiction (for example, proving that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational) is the fourth method, and belongs to the second year of the course. You do not need it yet.
2 The Language You Need

The subject content expects you to understand and use the standard notation of logic and sets. These symbols appear in exam questions and you should use them in your own proofs.

Is $0$ a natural number? Books disagree. Most UK A Level texts take $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, but some include $0$. If it matters to your proof, say which you mean — for example "for all integers $n \geqslant 1$".
The sets sit inside each other: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$. Every natural number is an integer, every integer is rational ($5 = \tfrac51$), and every rational number is real. Numbers such as $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$ are real but irrational.

Implication goes one way

"$A \Rightarrow B$" does not mean "$B \Rightarrow A$". The reverse statement is called the converse, and it may be false.

Worked Example 1 — Choosing the right arrow

Write $\Rightarrow$, $\Leftarrow$ or $\Leftrightarrow$ between each pair of statements.

①$x = 4 \quad \square \quad x^2 = 16$.  If $x = 4$ then certainly $x^2 = 16$; but $x^2 = 16$ could mean $x = -4$. So $x = 4 \Rightarrow x^2 = 16$.
②$x^3 = 8 \quad \square \quad x = 2$.  For real $x$ the cube root is unique, so each implies the other: $x^3 = 8 \Leftrightarrow x = 2$.
③$n$ is a multiple of $4$ $\quad \square \quad$ $n$ is even.  Every multiple of $4$ is even, but $6$ is even and not a multiple of $4$. So "$n$ is a multiple of $4$" $\Rightarrow$ "$n$ is even".
④$(x-1)(x-5) = 0 \quad \square \quad x = 1 \text{ or } x = 5$.  Both directions hold: $\Leftrightarrow$.
Test each direction separately. To reject an arrow, find one value that makes the left true and the right false.
3 Writing a Proof Properly

Examiners mark proofs on completeness and clarity, not just on whether the algebra is right. A good proof follows the same pattern every time.

  1. State your assumptions. Define every letter: "Let $n$ be any integer." "Let the two odd numbers be $2m+1$ and $2n+1$, where $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$."
  2. Work in logical steps. Each line must follow from the ones before. Use $\Rightarrow$ or words such as "so" and "therefore" to link them.
  3. Use general letters, not numbers. A proof about "any odd number" must work for $2n + 1$, not just for $7$.
  4. Reach the exact statement you were asked to prove. If you need to show something is a multiple of $3$, your last line must be of the form $3 \times (\text{an integer})$.
  5. Write a concluding sentence. For example: "Therefore the sum of any two odd numbers is even." Many mark schemes give the final mark only for this.
Standard forms for integers ($n \in \mathbb{Z}$)
Even: $2n$  ·  Odd: $2n + 1$  ·  Multiple of $k$: $kn$
Consecutive integers: $n,\; n+1,\; n+2$  ·  Consecutive odd numbers: $2n+1,\; 2n+3$
Worked Example 2 — A complete proof by deduction

Prove that the sum of any two odd numbers is even.

①Let the two odd numbers be $2m + 1$ and $2n + 1$, where $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
②Their sum is $(2m + 1) + (2n + 1) = 2m + 2n + 2$.
③$= 2(m + n + 1)$
④$m + n + 1$ is an integer, so the sum is $2 \times$ an integer.
⑤Therefore the sum of any two odd numbers is even. $\blacksquare$
Using $2n + 1$ for both numbers would only prove that an odd number added to itself is even. Two different numbers need two different letters.
Worked Example 3 — Proving an identity

Prove that $(n + 3)^2 - (n - 3)^2 \equiv 12n$.

①Start with the left-hand side only: $(n+3)^2 - (n-3)^2$
②$= \left(n^2 + 6n + 9\right) - \left(n^2 - 6n + 9\right)$
③$= 12n$, which is the right-hand side. $\blacksquare$
Work on one side. To prove an identity, start from one side (usually the more complicated) and transform it into the other. Do not write the identity down and "do the same to both sides" — that assumes what you are trying to prove.

5 Common Mistakes to Avoid
Proof by example. "$3 + 5 = 8$, $7 + 9 = 16$, so the sum of two odd numbers is even" is not a proof. Examples can suggest a result and can disprove one, but they can never prove a general statement.
Assuming what you want to prove. Starting with the statement and working until you reach "$0 = 0$" proves nothing unless every step is reversible — and it is very easy to lose marks this way. Start from what you know.
Mixing up $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$. $x = -2 \Rightarrow x^2 = 4$ is true, but $x^2 = 4 \Rightarrow x = -2$ is false. Only use $\Leftrightarrow$ when both directions really hold.
Dividing by something that might be zero. From $x^2 = 3x$, dividing by $x$ gives $x = 3$ and loses the solution $x = 0$. Factorise instead: $x(x - 3) = 0$.
Missing out the conclusion. Reaching $2(m + n + 1)$ is not the end. Say that $m + n + 1$ is an integer, and state clearly what this proves.
6 Quick Reference

$A \Rightarrow B$

If $A$ is true then $B$ is true. Says nothing about the converse.

$A \Leftrightarrow B$

$A$ and $B$ are either both true or both false.

Number sets

$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$

Even / odd

$2n$ and $2n+1$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Deduction

General algebra from known facts to the result.

Exhaustion

Finitely many cases — check every one.

Counter-example

One failure is enough to disprove a claim.

Identity

Start from one side; transform it into the other.

Finish

End with a sentence stating what has been proved.

7 Practice Questions

Ten questions drawing on the whole Proof topic. Work each one through before opening the solution.

Question 1

For each number, list every set from $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ that it belongs to:   (a) $-6$   (b) $0.\dot{3}$   (c) $\sqrt{5}$   (d) $\sqrt{49}$

▶ Show solution

(a) $-6 \in \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}$ (negative, so not in $\mathbb{N}$).

(b) $0.\dot{3} = \tfrac13$, so $0.\dot{3} \in \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}$.

(c) $\sqrt{5}$ is irrational: $\sqrt{5} \in \mathbb{R}$ only.

(d) $\sqrt{49} = 7$, so it belongs to all four: $\mathbb{N}, \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}$.

Question 2

Write $\Rightarrow$, $\Leftarrow$ or $\Leftrightarrow$ in each box.   (a) $x > 5 \;\square\; x > 2$   (b) $x^2 - 7x + 10 = 0 \;\square\; x = 2 \text{ or } x = 5$   (c) $x = -3 \;\square\; x^2 = 9$

▶ Show solution

(a) $\Rightarrow$. Any number bigger than $5$ is bigger than $2$, but $x = 3$ satisfies $x > 2$ and not $x > 5$.

(b) $\Leftrightarrow$, since $x^2 - 7x + 10 = (x-2)(x-5)$.

(c) $\Rightarrow$. $x = -3$ gives $x^2 = 9$, but $x^2 = 9$ could mean $x = 3$.

Question 3

Write down the converse of "If $n$ is a multiple of $6$, then $n$ is a multiple of $3$." Is the converse true?

▶ Show solution

Converse: "If $n$ is a multiple of $3$, then $n$ is a multiple of $6$."

This is false: $n = 9$ is a multiple of $3$ but not of $6$. (The original statement is true: $n = 6k = 3(2k)$.)

Question 4

Prove that the product of any two odd numbers is odd.

▶ Show solution

Let the numbers be $2m + 1$ and $2n + 1$ with $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

$(2m+1)(2n+1) = 4mn + 2m + 2n + 1 = 2(2mn + m + n) + 1$

$2mn + m + n$ is an integer, so the product has the form $2k + 1$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Therefore the product of any two odd numbers is odd.

Question 5

Prove that $(2n + 1)^2 - (2n - 1)^2$ is a multiple of $8$ for every integer $n$.

▶ Show solution

$(2n+1)^2 - (2n-1)^2 = \left(4n^2 + 4n + 1\right) - \left(4n^2 - 4n + 1\right) = 8n$

$n$ is an integer, so $8n$ is a multiple of $8$.

Therefore the difference of the squares of any two consecutive odd numbers is a multiple of $8$.

Question 6

Prove that $x^2 - 6x + 10 > 0$ for all real values of $x$.

▶ Show solution

Complete the square: $x^2 - 6x + 10 \equiv (x - 3)^2 - 9 + 10 = (x-3)^2 + 1$.

For all real $x$, $(x - 3)^2 \geqslant 0$, so $(x - 3)^2 + 1 \geqslant 1 > 0$.

Therefore $x^2 - 6x + 10 > 0$ for all real $x$.

Question 7

A student writes: "$x^2 = 5x \;\Rightarrow\; x = 5$. So the only solution of $x^2 = 5x$ is $x = 5$." Explain the error and give the correct conclusion.

▶ Show solution

The student has divided both sides by $x$, which is only valid if $x \neq 0$. The case $x = 0$ has been lost.

Correctly: $x^2 - 5x = 0 \Leftrightarrow x(x - 5) = 0 \Leftrightarrow x = 0 \text{ or } x = 5$.

Question 8

Prove by exhaustion that no square number ends in the digit $7$.

▶ Show solution

The last digit of $n^2$ depends only on the last digit of $n$. There are ten cases:

Last digit of $n$0123456789
Last digit of $n^2$0149656941

In every case the last digit is $0, 1, 4, 5, 6$ or $9$ — never $7$.

Since every integer falls into exactly one of these cases, no square number ends in $7$.

Question 9

Disprove the statement "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for every positive integer $n$."

▶ Show solution

Try $n = 40$: $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2$.

$1681 = 41 \times 41$ is not prime, so $n = 40$ is a counter-example and the statement is false.

(The formula does give primes for $n = 1, 2, \dots, 39$ — a famous warning that many examples do not make a proof. $n = 41$ also works as a counter-example: $41^2 + 41 + 41 = 41 \times 43$.)

Question 10

(a) Prove that the sum of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of $3$.

(b) Prove that the sum of any four consecutive integers is never a multiple of $4$.

(c) A student claims that "the sum of any $k$ consecutive integers is a multiple of $k$." Disprove this claim.

▶ Show solution

(a) Let the integers be $n, n+1, n+2$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Sum $= 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1)$, and $n + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so the sum is a multiple of $3$.

(b) Let the integers be $n, n+1, n+2, n+3$.

Sum $= 4n + 6 = 4(n + 1) + 2$.

This is $2$ more than a multiple of $4$, so it always leaves remainder $2$ when divided by $4$. Therefore it is never a multiple of $4$.

(c) Part (b) already shows the claim fails for $k = 4$: for example $1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10$, which is not a multiple of $4$.

A simpler counter-example is $k = 2$: $1 + 2 = 3$, which is not a multiple of $2$. One counter-example is enough, so the claim is false.
